Board of Zoning Appeals

Zoning appeal fee for many homeowners would rise to $500 from $100

Board backs its first fee update in 25 to 30 years; Metro Council has the final say

NASHVILLE — A homeowner who wants an exception to Metro's zoning code, for a narrow, shallow or steep lot that won't take a standard addition, pays $100 today to have the case heard. Under a fee package the Metro Board of Zoning Appeals endorsed Oct. 1, many of those residential appeals would cost $500.

The board, whose chairman is Ross Pepper, approved the schedule by voice vote and agreed to send it to Metro Council, which has final authority. Staff suggested the new fees take effect the following Jan. 1.

Staff told the board the fees had not changed in roughly 25 to 30 years. Residential variances would rise to as much as $500, and commercial and multifamily appeals would move to higher tiers. The meeting record does not give a revenue estimate.

Metro's posted schedule charges $100 for residential and $200 for non-residential variances and special exceptions on projects with no construction or valuations below its base fees, and $100 for administrative appeals.

Staff said higher fees are meant to discourage applicants from using appeals in place of designing projects to meet the code. Staff compared Nashville's fees with those in Austin and Charlotte and said Metro did not intend to match Austin's higher charges.

Homeowners on low or fixed incomes could get a reduced fee or a waiver, decided case by case. Staff also described a one-time free appeal that a Council member could grant. The board's legal counsel advised that fees must be related to the cost of implementation.

The board grants exceptions when the code doesn't fit a property; Metro's variance form lists hardships such as a lot's narrowness, shallowness, irregular shape or topography. On the same afternoon the board approved three requests on its consent agenda by voice vote, with no opposition present: setbacks for a new house on Burton Valley Road, an addition to a nonconforming structure on Marlin Court and a covered porch on Cahal Avenue.

It also granted a variance for a detached garage on North New Hope Road that had been built without a permit about 6.55 feet from the right-of-way, citing the lot's topography. The approval requires the owner to follow sight-distance recommendations during permitting; a neighbor had testified about visibility near a school bus stop.

Not yet public: the full peer-city figures, the income limit for a waiver, the commercial amounts and a date for Council action. Metro has not said whether appeals filed before the Council acts would pay the current fee. The Daily News will publish the draft ordinance when it is filed.

Before it's final: Metro Council must adopt the fees, and a check of the Council's legislative records found no fee ordinance filed at press time. For most appeals, applicants must start a building permit application before filing. The next filing deadline is Oct. 19, for the Dec. 3 hearing, and payment is due the Friday after the deadline. Written comments on a case are due by 4 p.m. the Thursday before its hearing. The board next meets Oct. 15 at 1 p.m. in the Sonny West Conference Center, Howard Office Building, 700 President Ronald Reagan Way. Questions go to BZA@nashville.gov.

The record: Watch the Oct. 1 meeting (Metro Nashville Network) · Oct. 1 agenda, item "Discussion of Board of Zoning Appeals Fee Change" · BZA fee schedule, 2026 filing calendar · Citizen Portal meeting summaries: fees, consent cases

The board also noted the death of former member Tom Lawless, remembered at the meeting as a longtime advocate for trees.

  1. Start a permit. For most appeals, a building permit application must be started first. Appeals of a zoning administrator's decision (Item A) are the exception.

  3. Pay the fee. Payment is due the Friday after the filing deadline. Today's fee is $100 for residential and $200 for non-residential cases with no construction or low valuations, and $100 for administrative appeals. Payment questions: codespermitpay@nashville.gov.
  4. If you are a neighbor. Written comments are due by 4 p.m. the Thursday before the hearing. Hearings start at 1 p.m. in the Sonny West Conference Center, Howard Office Building.

Metro Council

Metro would borrow up to $544.7 million for schools, roads and public buildings

Property taxes would repay the bonds; a vote is expected to wait for a later meeting

Metro would borrow up to $544,720,000 to pay for the mayor's proposed capital spending plan for fiscal 2027 under a resolution on tonight's Metro Council agenda.

The bonds would be general obligations repaid from property taxes on all taxable property in the General and Urban Services districts and backed by Metro's full faith and credit, the resolution says. It caps the interest rate at 7.25%.

General government projects would get $418,319,000 and Metro Nashville Public Schools $107,400,000, with $19,001,000 set aside for contingencies and the 1% for the Arts program.

The largest items include $48.98 million for road repair, $39 million for planning and site purchases for new Metro facilities, $35 million toward a new Davidson County Sheriff's Office facility and $30 million for a combined Fire Station No. 2, headquarters and Metro office project.

For schools, the largest pieces are $27.22 million for heating, air-conditioning and plumbing work, $15 million for an Antioch-cluster elementary school that a supporting exhibit says will need $21.46 million more, and $7.25 million for roofs. Sidewalks get $7 million, traffic safety and calming $10.85 million, bikeways $8.5 million and Parks $14.36 million.

The resolution is sponsored by Council members Jason Spain and Brenda Gadd. No referendum is required unless a petition for an election is filed within the time and in the manner state law provides, it says.

A separate exhibit ranks the Council's own capital priorities. First is a new Metro Animal Care and Control facility, followed by a new Parkwood/Brick Church Pike library.

The resolution does not say what the borrowing would add to Metro's annual debt payments or whether it would affect the property tax rate.

Before it's final: Council staff wrote that the resolution will be deferred at tonight's meeting under Council Rule 21, so a vote would come later; the next regular meeting is Oct. 20 at 6:30 p.m. Tennessee residents may sign up to speak during public comment from 5 to 6 p.m. at a table outside the Council Chamber. The period runs 20 minutes, two minutes per speaker.

Tourism and Convention Commission

Hotel-tax collections hit a record of just over $158 million

Guests pay 7% of the room charge plus $2.50 a night; $45.9 million went to tourism promotion

Metro's hotel-tax collections reached a record of just over $158 million in the fiscal year that ended June 30, staff told the Metro Tourism and Convention Commission on Sept. 22.

The tax is charged to hotel guests and collected by hotel operators: 7% of the room charge plus $2.50 per room per night, according to Metro Finance. Of the total, $45.9 million went to the tourism promotion contract, staff said. The meeting record does not show how the rest was divided.

The commission approved the unaudited financial report for the year by voice vote. An outside audit by Baker Tilly is underway.

Commissioners re-elected Lisa LeClaire as chair and Mark Hayes as vice chair, welcomed new commissioner Darius McDonald and thanked outgoing commissioner Rob Higgins.

Bruce McGregor, presenting the commission's visitor research, said Nashville drew about 17.4 million visitors in 2025 who spent $11.6 billion in the county and generated about $1.2 billion in state and local taxes. The 2026 forecast is 17.8 million visitors and $12.2 billion.

Before it's final: The year's figures are unaudited until Baker Tilly finishes. The mayor's appointment of Tod Roadarmel to the commission, for a term ending June 30, 2028, is on tonight's Council agenda.

Parks

Parks plan would fix what Metro owns before adding more, through 2031

The repair backlog has grown, but no current dollar total is in the public record

Metro Parks' update to its "Plan to Play" master plan puts repairing existing parks ahead of expansion, Tim Naech, an assistant director in the department's planning division, told the Council's Arts, Parks, Libraries and Entertainment Committee on Sept. 15.

The plan rests on three funding tiers through 2031: flat funding that mainly stabilizes the current system, a middle tier that keeps service per resident where it is, and an expansion tier. The capital plan now before the Council includes $14,361,000 for Parks, with $4.6 million for greenways and $3.281 million for bridges and boardwalks.

Naech said the maintenance backlog has grown since the 2017 plan, but no current dollar figure appears in the meeting record. The department's ranked list for fiscal 2027 includes boardwalk and bridge replacements, community-center elevator repairs, playgrounds, removal of trees killed by the emerald ash borer and a funding shortfall for the Westbank Dock.

Parks has added about 600 acres since 2017, but acres per resident have fallen because the population grew faster, Naech said. Philanthropic partners have put in more than $28 million since 2021.

The Park Board recently adopted a policy for locally designated natural areas, and Naech said a Council ordinance could follow to give those sites more protection. The committee approved its consent items 5-0, including two greenway conservation easements.

Before it's final: The committee meets again today at 5:30 p.m. in Committee Room 4 of the Historic Metro Courthouse; its agenda includes a Centennial Sportsplex water main ordinance (BL2026-1573). Committee public comment uses a sign-up sheet outside the room: eight minutes in all, two minutes per speaker.

Schools

MNPS credit recovery takes 60% mastery, and the NCAA may not count it

District spells out who may change a grade; board's consent list carried a $145 million retiree health contract

Metro Nashville Public Schools students who fail a course can earn the credit back through credit recovery only if they reach 60% mastery, and the transcript will mark the course "CR" with the original grade still showing, Deputy Chief of Academics David Williams told a school board committee on Sept. 22.

Students must have attempted the course and shown at least 50% mastery to enroll. Williams said the NCAA eligibility clearinghouse may not accept recovered credits, so athletes who hope to play in college may need to retake the course.

A grade change must be requested in writing, with the reason recorded, by the teacher of record or the executive principal, and a principal must consult the teacher first. Disputes go to the executive director and then to the grievance process. Williams cited state law and State Board of Education policy 3.301.

The full board's consent agenda that evening listed 33 contracts and agreements. The largest was up to $145 million through 2031 for United Healthcare to provide medical and prescription coverage for Medicare-eligible MNPS retirees, about $29 million a year from the district's Insurance Trust Fund. Others included up to $7.12 million over five years for an InSite Health behavioral-health and threat-assessment platform, and up to $1,461,518 for Lipscomb University to cover tuition for support staff training to become teachers.

Minutes recording the board's votes had not been posted on the district's website at press time.

Before it's final: The Board of Education, chaired by Freda Player, next meets Oct. 27 at 5 p.m. Speakers during public participation are asked to keep remarks to two minutes. The board's Ethics Committee meets Wednesday, Oct. 7, at 10 a.m. in the Board Room.

Downtown

Downtown owners' extra assessment pays for cleaning, safety and shuttles

Partnership tells the tourism commission downtown yields about 12% of county property taxes

Downtown Nashville generates about 12% of Davidson County's property taxes and roughly $15 billion in economic output, Tom Turner of the Nashville Downtown Partnership told the Metro Tourism and Convention Commission on Sept. 22.

Owners of taxable real property in the downtown Central Business Improvement District pay a special assessment of $0.001294 per dollar of assessed value, about $1.29 per $1,000, under Metro ordinance BL2025-846. Retailers in the district also collect a fee of 0.5% on certain taxable sales, according to the state Department of Revenue. The ordinance puts the initial cost of the district's services at $8,014,963.

The Partnership says about 67% of assessments go to cleaning and safety, 17% to economic development, 7% to image and communications and 9% to administration, and all fee revenue goes to cleaning and safety. Turner described three shuttle routes with about 500 monthly pass holders and about three miles of plantings on 11 streets, with 54,000 plants set out each year.

The rate can change only if the district's management corporation asks, the Council holds a public hearing and the Council passes an ordinance. The Gulch Business Improvement District becomes part of the downtown district in 2027, the Partnership says.

The meeting record does not show how the 12% figure was calculated.

What's next: No change is pending. The ordinance requires the Partnership to report at least quarterly to the Tourism and Convention Commission and Metro Finance.

Editorial

Don't price neighbors out of their own hearing

A zoning code is written for the typical lot, and Nashville is full of lots that aren't typical: the narrow ones, the steep ones, the odd-shaped parcels that older streets left behind. The Board of Zoning Appeals exists so that a homeowner with one of them can explain why the rule doesn't fit and ask for relief. It is the code's own safety valve.

Its staff now wants to raise the price of using that valve to $500 for many residential cases, from $100. Some increase is fair. The fee has not changed in a quarter-century, and the waiver for low-income homeowners shows the board understands who gets hurt by a flat charge.

But look at the reason staff gave: too many people use appeals instead of designing to the code. If that is true, the problem is the code. On the same afternoon, the board granted three variances on its consent agenda, by voice vote, with no one there to oppose them. When a city grants the same exceptions routinely, it is making residents ask permission for things it has already decided to allow. Raising the toll on that request doesn't fix the rule. It sorts applicants by who can afford to ask.

And the waiver leaves out the middle. The family stretching its budget to add a room for an aging parent won't qualify as low-income, and it will pay the full $500 before anyone has looked at its plans.

The board's own lawyer told it that fees must be related to the cost of implementation. That is the right test, and the public hasn't been shown the numbers to apply it.

Before Metro Council votes, it should ask for three things. Publish what a hearing actually costs Metro, and set the fee near that cost. Publish the peer-city comparison the board heard. And ask Planning which sections of the code generate the most routine variances, so those rules can be fixed for everyone instead of priced for each applicant.
